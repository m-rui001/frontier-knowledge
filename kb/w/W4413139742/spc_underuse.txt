Introduction
Hypertension affects nearly half of U.S. adults and remains a leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease.
1
Despite effective therapies, blood pressure control rates remain suboptimal nationwide.
2
Single-pill combinations (SPCs), which combine two antihypertensive agents into a single formulation, are recommended to to simplify treatment and improve adherence.
3
Clinical guidelines increasingly emphasize this approach. The 2017 hypertension guideline recommended SPCs for patients requiring combination treatment.
4
More recent 2025 guideline have further elevated this recommendation, identifying SPCs as a preferred initial strategy for stage 2 hypertension because combination therapy can achieve faster blood pressure reduction and reduce therapeutic inertia.
3
,
5
Despite these recommendations, real-world adoption of SPC therapy remains uncertain, as prior studies relied on limited or outdated data. Using a large national electronic health record (EHR) network, we examined contemporary SPC initiation among adults with newly diagnosed hypertension in the U.S. from 2015 to 2025.
Methods
We conducted a retrospective observational study using the Epic Cosmos database, a large national EHR platform covering over 300 million patients. We identified adults with newly diagnosed hypertension (identified via ICD-10-CM codes I10-I15) between 2015–2025 who initiated antihypertensive pharmacotherapy and had ⩾1 year of observable history.
Eligible patients were adults initiating treatment with a major antihypertensive drug classes (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), or diuretics). The index date was defined as the earliest antihypertensive prescription date within 30 days following the initial diagnosis. Individuals with prior hypertension diagnoses, prior antihypertensive prescriptions, or concurrent ACEI and ARB prescriptions on the index date were excluded. SPCs were defined as fixed-dose combinations of ⩾2 antihypertensive agents, whereas separate-pill combinations involved ⩾2 distinct agents prescribed individually. The primary outcome was SPC initiation, assessed quarterly from 2015–2025.
To assess temporal trends in prescribing rates, we estimated the average annual percent change (AAPC) using a generalized linear model with a Poisson distribution and logarithmic link, incorporating the quarterly number of patients as an offset. We conducted stratified analyses by drug class, primary insurance, baseline systolic blood pressure (SBP), race and ethnicity, and neighborhood-level Social Vulnerability Index (SVI). Baseline SBP was defined as the highest measurement within 90 days before the index date. Sensitivity analyses were restricted to patients with confirmed stage 2 hypertension, defined as at least two blood pressure measurements ⩾140/90 mmHg. All statistical tests were two-sided (P < .05). Analyses were conducted using Microsoft SQL Server and R version 4.5.2.
Results
Among 7,204,590 adults with newly diagnosed hypertension who initiated antihypertensive therapy, 423,560 patients (5.88%) initiated a SPCs, 561,223 (7.79%) a separate-pill combinations, and 6,219,807 (86.33%) monotherapy.

Patterns of SPC prescribing varied by drug class. ACEI–diuretic combinations accounted for the largest share of SPC prescriptions (2.22% in Q3 2022), but declined over time (AAPC −13.38% [−13.52 to −13.24],

Across clinical and sociodemographic groups, SPC initiation remained consistently low and declined over time. By baseline SBP, SPC use was paradoxically highest among patients with an SBP <130 mm Hg (7.09%) and lowest among those with 140–149 mmHg (4.78%). For insurance type, commercially insured patients had the highest rates of SPC use (7.05%), whereas those with Medicaid coverage had the lowest (4.08%). Across race and ethnicity, rates remained below 10.1% across all groups (range, 4.11%–10.1%). Similarly, SPC prescribing remained low across SVI quartiles, ranging from 5.79% (lowest quartile) to 6.11% (highest quartile).
Discussion
In this large national analysis of over 7 million adults with newly diagnosed hypertension, initiation of SPC therapy remained uncommon over the past decade, even among patients with stage 2 hypertension or across higher baseline SBP strata (140–149, 150–159, and ≥160 mmHg). Despite increasingly guideline-supported recommendations favoring combination therapy, fewer than one in ten patients initiating antihypertensive treatment received an SPC.
These findings highlight a persistent disconnect between guideline recommendations and routine clinical practice. The rationale for combination therapy is well established: most patients ultimately require more than one antihypertensive agent to achieve blood pressure targets, and SPCs can simplify treatment regimens, reduce pill burden, and improve adherence.
6
,
7
Initiating combination therapy may also accelerate blood pressure control compared with traditional stepwise escalation strategies.
3
,
8
Yet our findings suggest that this paradigm has not translated into widespread adoption. SPC use did not increase following the publication of major hypertension guidelines recommending combination therapy. Several factors may contribute to this persistent underuse.
3
Clinicians may remain accustomed to initiating treatment with a single agent and escalating gradually, and concerns about tolerability, dose-titration flexibility, and insurance or formulary constraints may also influence prescribing decisions. In addition, clinical workflows and electronic prescribing systems may not consistently support SPCs as an initial treatment option, rather than reflecting inherent limitations of SPCs. This pattern reflects a broader challenge in cardiovascular medicine: translating evidence-based recommendations into everyday practice.
9
Importantly, the consistently low use of SPC therapy across racial and ethnic groups and across levels of neighborhood SVI suggests that underutilization reflects systemic practice patterns rather than disparities confined to specific populations. However, the lower uptake among Medicaid versus commercially insured patients highlights persistent barriers to equitable access. Given the high burden of uncontrolled hypertension in the U.S., improving adoption of evidence-based treatment strategies represents an important opportunity to improve population cardiovascular health.
These findings underscore the need to move beyond guideline publication alone and focus on implementation.
9
Strategies such as clinical decision support tools, simplified treatment algorithms, and health system–level initiatives may help facilitate the routine use of combination therapy when clinically appropriate. As hypertension remains a major modifiable drivers of cardiovascular risk, closing this implementation gap could yield substantial public health impact.
What is the clinical question being addressed?
Among adults with newly diagnosed hypertension in the United States, how frequently is guideline-recommended single-pill combination therapy used as initial antihypertensive treatment?
What is the main finding?
In a national electronic health record analysis of nearly 9 million patients from 2015–2025, fewer than 1 in 10 patients initiating antihypertensive therapy received a single-pill combination, with no increase following major guideline recommendations.
