Abstract
Purpose of Review
This review examines the 2025 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology multisociety hypertension guideline, placing its recommendations in historical context and evaluating the supporting evidence across major clinical populations.
Recent Findings
The 2025 guideline retains the 2017 blood pressure categories and treatment goal of less than 130/80 mmHg but introduces the race-free PREVENT equations, uses a 7.5% 10-year risk threshold to guide pharmacologic treatment of stage-1 hypertension, and encourages systolic blood pressure below 120 mmHg in selected high-risk patients. Additional updates include single-pill combination therapy for stage-2 hypertension, broader primary aldosteronism screening, routine urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio measurement, removal of race-based drug selection, and cautious incorporation of renal denervation. Recent trials strengthen the evidence for intensive lowering, including in diabetes and older adults.
Summary
The 2025 guideline advances a risk-based, measurement-dependent approach. The principal challenge is no longer identifying effective treatments but delivering accurate measurement, timely intensification, and sustained follow-up at scale.
Keywords:
Hypertension, AHA/ACC guideline, PREVENT, Intensive treatment, Primary aldosteronism, Renal denervation
Introduction
Hypertension affects nearly half of U.S. adults and remains a major driver of myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, chronic kidney disease (CKD), cognitive impairment, and premature death [
1
]. During 2021–2023, 47.7% of U.S. adults met the contemporary definition of hypertension as blood pressure (BP) ≥ 130/80 mmHg, yet only about one-fifth achieved control below 130/80 mmHg [
2
]. Control using the traditional < 140/90 mmHg threshold has also shown no meaningful improvement in recent years, underscoring a persistent implementation gap despite effective and inexpensive therapies [
3
].
Against this backdrop, on August 14, 2025, the American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology (ACC), alongside 11 collaborating organizations, released the first comprehensive U.S. adult hypertension guideline since 2017 [
1
]. The update retains the 2017 BP categories and general treatment goal of < 130/80 mmHg but changes how clinicians estimate cardiovascular risk, initiate therapy, evaluate secondary causes and target-organ injury, and manage difficult-to-control hypertension. Major updates include adoption of PREVENT, earlier pharmacologic treatment of persistent lower-risk stage-1 hypertension, greater use of single-pill combination therapy, routine assessment of albuminuria, broader screening for primary aldosteronism, and selective incorporation of renal denervation.
This review places these recommendations in historical context, examines the evidence supporting contemporary treatment thresholds and targets, and discusses their application across major clinical populations. Particular attention is given to SBP < 120 mmHg, for which potential cardiovascular and cognitive benefits must be balanced against measurement quality, treatment tolerance, and individual clinical context.
From Stepped Care to Risk-Based Treatment
U.S. hypertension guidance has evolved from treating severe, predominantly diastolic hypertension to preventing cardiovascular disease across the continuum of BP and cardiovascular risk. Early reports from the Joint National Committee (JNC), which was appointed by the National Institute of Health, have defined the presence of severe hypertension based on diastolic BP alone as cardiovascular outcome data that pertain to systolic BP were not available [
4
–
7
]. By JNC 5 and JNC 6, evidence from clinical trials and epidemiological studies led to revision of the hypertension definition to 140/90 mmHg as the conventional diagnostic threshold, while treatment decisions increasingly incorporated target-organ damage and comorbid conditions [
8
,
9
].
JNC 7 also introduced “prehypertension” for SBP 120–139 mmHg or DBP 80–89 mmHg to identify patients at risk for progression and emphasize lifestyle intervention. It also recommended lower treatment goals for patients with diabetes or CKD [
10
]. Subsequently, the NIH has shifted the task in providing hypertension treatment recommendation to the AHA and the ACC. Nevertheless, in 2014 the panel of JNC8 that had been appointed, provided the evidence-based panel report that recommended a threshold and goal of 150/90 mmHg for adults aged ≥ 60 years and 140/90 mmHg for younger adults and those with diabetes or CKD [
11
]. This recommendation generated substantial controversy. A minority of panel members argued that the evidence did not justify relaxing the SBP goal to 150 mmHg in otherwise healthy adults aged 60–79 years [
12
].
The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline represented the decisive shift toward the current risk-based framework. It defined hypertension as BP ≥ 130/80 mmHg, classified 130–139/80–89 mmHg as stage-1 hypertension, and recommended a treatment goal below 130/80 mmHg for most treated adults [
13
]. Although the revised definition substantially increased the estimated prevalence of hypertension, it did not imply that every newly classified patient required medication. Pharmacologic treatment of stage-1 hypertension was linked to established cardiovascular disease or elevated predicted cardiovascular risk, whereas lower-risk adults were initially managed with lifestyle modification and follow-up.
The 2025 ACC/AHA guideline preserves the 2017 BP categories and the overarching treatment goal of < 130/80 mmHg but further advances the transition toward risk-based and earlier treatment. The PREVENT equations replace the Pooled Cohort Equations, and a 10-year cardiovascular disease risk of ≥ 7.5% is now used to guide pharmacologic treatment among adults with stage-1 hypertension who do not already have cardiovascular disease, prior stroke, diabetes, or CKD [
1
]. Importantly, lower-risk adults with stage-1 hypertension are no longer managed with lifestyle modification indefinitely. If BP remains ≥ 130/80 mmHg after 3–6 months of structured lifestyle intervention, initiation of medication is recommended. Thus, the principal change from 2017 is not how hypertension is defined, but when persistent BP elevation warrants escalation from lifestyle treatment to pharmacotherapy. What’s new in the 2025 Guidelines
BP Classification, Confirmation, and Measurement
The 2017 BP categories remain unchanged: normal BP is defined as SBP < 120 mmHg and DBP < 80 mmHg; elevated BP as SBP 120–129 mmHg and DBP < 80 mmHg; stage-1 hypertension as SBP 130–139 mmHg or DBP 80–89 mmHg; and stage-2 hypertension as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg [
1
]. When SBP and DBP fall into different categories, the higher category applies. Importantly, these categories describe average BP rather than a single isolated reading.
Diagnosis should rely on averaged readings obtained with a validated upper-arm device, correct cuff size, supported posture, the arm at heart level, no talking, and adequate rest. Automated oscillometric devices facilitate repeated measurements but do not compensate for poor technique. Home or ambulatory monitoring should confirm hypertension when feasible, identify white-coat and masked hypertension, and guide titration. Home monitoring is most effective when paired with standardized protocols and regular clinical follow-up. Cuffless devices, including smartwatches, should not currently be used to diagnose hypertension or direct treatment because their precision and reliability remain insufficient.
PREVENT Replaces the Pooled Cohort Equations
The guideline replaces the Pooled Cohort Equations with the AHA Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease EVENTs (PREVENT) equations. PREVENT was developed and externally validated in more than 6.6 million U.S. adults and provides 10- and 30-year estimates of total cardiovascular disease, including atherosclerotic events and heart failure, in adults aged 30–79 years without known cardiovascular disease [
14
]. The equations are sex-specific and race-free, incorporate routine cardiovascular, metabolic, and kidney variables, and allow optional inclusion of hemoglobin A1c, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), and the social deprivation index.
For stage-1 hypertension, increased risk is defined as a 10-year PREVENT cardiovascular disease risk ≥ 7.5% [
1
]. Although numerically lower than the former 10% Pooled Cohort Equations threshold, the 7.5% value was selected to produce broadly comparable treatment implications with the new model rather than simply lower the threshold for treatment. The 7.5% threshold is also deemed to have equivalent risk prediction as the Framingham risk score threshold of 15% which is one of the eligibility criteria for the SPRINT trial. PREVENT is most useful in patients without clinical cardiovascular disease, prior stroke, diabetes, or CKD.
When to Start Medication and How Low to Aim
The overarching treatment goal remains < 130/80 mmHg for most adults with hypertension [
1
]. Among patients at increased cardiovascular risk, the guideline recommends achieving an SBP < 130 mmHg and encourages further lowering to < 120 mmHg when feasible, tolerated, and measured using standardized techniques. The latter should not be interpreted as a universal target for every patient. For adults at lower cardiovascular risk, SBP < 130 mmHg remains the principal goal, although an SBP < 120 mmHg may be reasonable in appropriately selected patients. Treatment targets should be individualized for patients who are pregnant, reside in institutional care, have a limited predicted lifespan, experience symptomatic orthostatic hypotension, or have substantial frailty or treatment-related adverse effects.
Treatment thresholds remain distinct from treatment goals. All adults with an average BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg should receive lifestyle counseling and antihypertensive medication, irrespective of predicted cardiovascular risk. Adults with BP of 130–139/80–89 mmHg should begin medication if they have clinical cardiovascular disease, prior stroke, diabetes, CKD, or a 10-year PREVENT risk ≥ 7.5%. Lower-risk adults should initially receive a structured lifestyle intervention. If their average BP remains ≥ 130/80 mmHg after three to six months, medication is recommended rather than continued observation without a defined endpoint. This is an important change from the 2017 framework, which allowed lower-risk stage-1 hypertension to be managed with nonpharmacologic therapy and periodic reassessment without an explicit requirement to begin medication.
Simpler, Earlier Pharmacotherapy
For adults with stage-2 hypertension, initial treatment with two first-line agents from different classes is preferred, ideally administered as a fixed-dose single-pill combination. The principal first-line classes remain an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), a long-acting dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker, and a thiazide-type diuretic. A renin–angiotensin system blocker combined with either a calcium-channel blocker or a thiazide-type diuretic is a common initial regimen. ACE inhibitors and ARBs should not be used together. Beta-blockers remain appropriate when there is a compelling indication, such as ischemic heart disease, selected arrhythmias, or heart failure, but are not routine first-line therapy for uncomplicated hypertension.
The preference for a single pill is practical rather than cosmetic. Fixed-dose combinations reduce pill burden, improve adherence and persistence, and shorten the time required to achieve BP control compared with sequential monotherapy [
15
]. Beginning with complementary agents at lower doses may also produce greater BP reduction with fewer class-specific adverse effects than maximizing a single drug before adding another. More importantly, many 2-in-1 and even certain 3-in-1 combination pills have become generic such that the cost of co-payment is substantially lower, and in some instances, cheaper than the cost of 2–3 free equivalent pills.
Another meaningful change is the removal of race-based drug selection. Unlike the 2017 guideline, the 2025 guideline does not recommend a distinct first-line regimen solely on the basis of Black race. Drug selection should be guided by comorbidities, target-organ involvement, prior treatment response, adverse-effect risk, medication access, and patient preference [
1
]. This race-neutral approach doesn’t minimize the profound racial inequities in hypertension prevalence, treatment, and control [
16
]. Rather, it avoids using a social classification as a surrogate for an individual pharmacologic phenotype.
Lifestyle Treatment and Cardiometabolic Therapy
Lifestyle therapy remains foundational across the entire BP spectrum and should be prescribed with the same specificity as pharmacotherapy. The guideline recommends a DASH-style eating pattern; reduction of dietary sodium intake to < 2,300 mg/day, ideally approaching 1,500 mg/day; weight loss of at least 5% in adults with overweight or obesity; regular aerobic and resistance exercise; and moderate potassium intake, preferably from dietary sources, when kidney function and concomitant medications permit [
1
]. Potassium-enriched salt substitutes may provide additional BP and cardiovascular benefit, particularly when sodium intake arises largely from food preparation at home but require caution in patients with CKD or those taking medications that impair potassium excretion [
17
]. Abstinence from alcohol is preferred; adults who continue to drink should limit intake to no more than one standard drink daily for women or two for men. Isometric resistance exercise and stress-management practices, including meditation, may be reasonable adjuncts but should not displace established lifestyle measures or indicated medications.
The guideline also places BP management within a broader cardiometabolic framework. In adults with hypertension and overweight or obesity, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist may be considered when otherwise clinically appropriate. Its principal role is to promote weight loss and reduce cardiometabolic risk, with an accompanying reduction in BP, rather than to replace conventional antihypertensive therapy. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors likewise produce modest BP reductions and provide substantial cardiorenal benefit in selected patients with diabetes, CKD, or heart failure, although they are not classified as first-line antihypertensive agents.
Kidney Assessment and Secondary Hypertension
Measurement of UACR is now recommended as part of the routine initial evaluation of adults with hypertension, complementing standard laboratory testing and electrocardiography [
1
]. This addition is clinically consequential: albuminuria can identify previously unrecognized kidney injury, refine cardiovascular and kidney risk, and influence the selection and intensity of therapy.
Primary aldosteronism receives substantially greater attention because it remains common, frequently normokalemic, and markedly underdiagnosed [
18
,
19
]. Screening is recommended in patients with resistant hypertension regardless of potassium level, spontaneous or diuretic-induced hypokalemia, obstructive sleep apnea, an adrenal incidentaloma, or a family history of hypertension beginning at a young age or stroke occurring before age 40; screening may also be considered in all adults with stage-2 hypertension [
1
]. This broader approach is supported by evidence that primary aldosteronism occurs across the BP spectrum and becomes more prevalent as hypertension severity increases [
20
,
21
].
Screening can generally be performed while most antihypertensive medications are continued, reducing an important practical barrier. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and other strongly interfering agents may require individualized adjustment. When suspicion remains high despite negative or equivocal results, hypokalemia and medication interference should be addressed before repeat testing. The guideline also emphasizes evaluation for renal parenchymal and renovascular disease, sleep apnea, other endocrine disorders, and medications or substances that raise BP.
Resistant Hypertension and Renal Denervation
Apparent treatment-resistant hypertension should first prompt confirmation of standardized office and out-of-office BP, medication adherence, maximally tolerated dosing, interfering substances, and secondary causes. Once pseudoresistance has been excluded, pharmacotherapy should generally include complementary long-acting agents, an effective thiazide-like diuretic when kidney function permits, and a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist when not contraindicated. PATHWAY-2 established spironolactone as the most effective fourth-line agent [
22
].
Catheter-based renal denervation is now a Class 2b adjunctive option for carefully selected adults with resistant hypertension that remains uncontrolled despite optimized lifestyle and pharmacologic therapy, or in patients unable to tolerate multiple medications, after informed shared decision-making [
1
]. Candidate selection should occur within an experienced multidisciplinary program after confirmation of true resistant hypertension, exclusion of treatable secondary causes, and assessment of kidney function and renal artery anatomy.
Sham-controlled trials suggest that renal denervation lowers 24-hour ambulatory SBP by approximately 3–6 mmHg, with substantial individual variability [
23
]. The effect is therefore clinically relevant but generally modest, and the procedure should not be presented as curative or as a routine substitute for medication. Moreover, no randomized renal-denervation trial has yet demonstrated reductions in myocardial infarction, stroke, heart-failure events, or mortality.
Severe Hypertension and Acute-Care Settings
The replacement of “hypertensive urgency” with “severe hypertension” emphasizes that emergency treatment depends on acute target-organ injury, not BP alone. In nonpregnant adults, severe hypertension is defined as SBP > 180 mmHg and/or DBP > 120 mmHg without acute target-organ damage [
1
,
24
]. Clinicians should repeat the measurement and evaluate for neurologic, cardiac, aortic, retinal, and kidney injury.
Hypertensive emergency remains defined by severe BP elevation accompanied by acute target-organ injury and requires monitored treatment, usually intravenously. In contrast, patients without acute injury should not undergo reflexive rapid BP reduction. Those presenting from the outpatient setting usually require timely initiation, reinitiation, or intensification of oral therapy, identification of contributing factors, and close follow-up.
A similar principle applies to patients hospitalized for unrelated conditions who have asymptomatic severe BP elevation. Intermittent intravenous medication or short-acting oral therapy should not be administered solely to normalize an isolated BP value. Clinicians should address pain, anxiety, medication omission, withdrawal, volume disturbance, or other reversible contributors, and adjust the long-term regimen when appropriate. Pregnancy, acute ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and other condition-specific emergencies use separate thresholds and management pathways.
Evidence for More Intensive SBP Lowering
Earlier trials established that treating systolic hypertension prevents cardiovascular events. In SHEP, chlorthalidone-based treatment of isolated systolic hypertension reduced stroke by 36% and heart failure by approximately half [
25
]. SPRINT then provided pivotal U.S. evidence for a lower target by randomizing 9,361 adults aged ≥ 50 years with increased cardiovascular risk, but without diabetes or prior stroke, to SBP targets < 120 or < 140 mmHg. Intensive treatment reduced the primary cardiovascular composite by 25% and all-cause mortality by 27%; the final adjudicated analysis produced similar estimates [
26
,
27
]. Hypotension, electrolyte abnormalities, acute kidney injury, and syncope occurred more frequently, underscoring the need for careful selection and monitoring.
Evidence in diabetes was initially uncertain. ACCORD-BP did not significantly reduce its primary cardiovascular composite with an SBP target < 120 mmHg, although stroke was reduced by 41% [
28
]. Interpretation was limited by an event rate approximately half that anticipated. BPROAD subsequently randomized 12,821 high-risk Chinese adults with type 2 diabetes and found a 21% reduction in the primary cardiovascular outcome with intensive treatment [
29
]. Serious adverse events overall were similar, although symptomatic hypotension and hyperkalemia were more frequent. Together, these trials support intensive treatment in appropriately selected high-risk patients with diabetes.
STEP provided further evidence by randomizing 8,511 Chinese adults aged 60–80 years. Targeting SBP 110 to < 130 rather than 130 to < 150 mmHg reduced the primary cardiovascular composite by 26% [
30
]. During extended follow-up, all participants were offered intensive treatment, yet those originally assigned to the intensive group retained a lower event rate, supporting earlier and sustained treatment [
31
]. ESPRIT enrolled 11,255 high-risk adults, including patients with diabetes or prior stroke, and found a more modest 12% reduction with a target < 120 rather than < 140 mmHg, although syncope was more frequent [
32
].
Viewed collectively, these trials support a graded relationship between SBP reduction and cardiovascular benefit rather than a single universally optimal threshold. An individual-participant meta-analysis found that each 5-mmHg SBP reduction lowered major cardiovascular events by approximately 10%, with broadly consistent relative benefit across baseline BP levels and among patients with and without prior cardiovascular disease [
33
]. Absolute benefit is greatest at higher baseline risk, whereas treatment burden and adverse effects become more important in lower-risk or frail patients.
Translation requires standardized measurement. SPRINT used an automated protocol with five minutes of seated rest and the average of three readings, but staff presence varied without materially changing achieved BP or treatment effects [
34
]. A fixed conversion asserting that an SBP of 120 mmHg in SPRINT invariably corresponds to 130–135 mmHg in routine practice is unsupported. Intensive targets should instead be pursued with validated devices, standardized technique, and appropriate safety monitoring. Special Populations
Older Adults and Cognitive Health
Chronologic age alone is not a reason to accept poorly controlled SBP. The guideline recommends SBP < 130 mmHg for most older adults and encourages lower values in those at elevated cardiovascular risk when treatment is well tolerated [
1
]. Functional status, frailty, orthostatic symptoms, falls, polypharmacy, and goals of care should guide individualization.
Evidence that BP lowering preserves cognitive health has strengthened. SPRINT-MIND did not significantly reduce probable dementia alone, but it reduced mild cognitive impairment and the combined outcome of mild cognitive impairment or probable dementia [
35
]. During extended follow-up, benefit for the combined outcome persisted, whereas probable dementia alone remained nonsignificant [
36
].
The China Rural Hypertension Control Project extended this evidence beyond cognitive composites. Among 33,995 adults aged ≥ 40 years with uncontrolled hypertension in rural China, a community healthcare provider–led intervention targeting BP < 130/80 mmHg produced a net SBP reduction of 22 mmHg over 48 months and reduced all-cause dementia by 15%, with fewer serious adverse events [
37
]. Although the large BP separation and community-based care model differ from conventional target-comparison trials, this was the first large, randomized trial to demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in all-cause dementia with an intensive BP intervention. An individual-participant meta-analysis of five placebo-controlled trials likewise found a 13% reduction in incident dementia with antihypertensive treatment [
38
]. Taken together, the evidence now supports not only reductions in mild cognitive impairment and composite cognitive outcomes but also a probable reduction in dementia itself. The benefit appears most likely to emerge when meaningful BP reduction is achieved and sustained over several years.
Pregnancy and Postpartum Care
Historically, antihypertensive treatment in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy was reserved for BP ≥ 160/110 mm Hg, with a goal below 160/105 mm Hg, owing to concerns that lower targets could compromise placental perfusion and fetal growth. The Chronic Hypertension and Pregnancy trial randomized 2,408 patients with mild chronic hypertension before 23 weeks of gestation to active treatment targeting BP < 140/90 mm Hg or to receive no such treatment unless severe hypertension developed (SBP ≥ 160 mmHg or DBP ≥ 105 mmHg). Active treatment reduced a composite of severe preeclampsia, medically indicated preterm birth before 35 weeks, placental abruption, or fetal or neonatal death without increasing small-for-gestational-age birth [
39
]. These findings established that treating mild chronic hypertension improves pregnancy outcomes without an apparent fetal-growth penalty.
Following publication of CHAP in 2022, both the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine (SMFM) updated their recommendations to initiate or titrate antihypertensive therapy for chronic hypertension in pregnancy at 140/90 mm Hg; SMFM additionally recommended treatment to a goal < 140/90 mm Hg. These recommendations are supported by the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline [
40
,
41
]. However, uncertainty exists in regard to target BP in gestational hypertension and preeclampsia due to lack of clinical trial data addressing these subgroups of HDP. Currently, the 2025 guideline does not provide specific recommendations addressing BP targets for the treatment of gestational hypertension and preeclampsia. Consistent with ACOG guidance, the guidelines concur with treatment when BP exceeds ≥ 160/110 mmHg threshold.
When treatment is indicated, labetalol and extended-release nifedipine are generally preferred. Methyldopa is considered an alternative as treatment is not as effective. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, direct renin inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists are generally avoided, and low-dose aspirin is recommended for women at high risk for development of preeclampsia, particularly among women with chronic hypertension to prevent preeclampsia and its complications. Early postpartum BP surveillance is essential because BP may rise after discharge. A hypertensive disorder of pregnancy should remain in the medical record as a marker of increased long-term cardiovascular risk.
Chronic Kidney Disease
CKD substantially increases both cardiovascular and kidney risk, making accurate measurement and treatment tolerance particularly important. For adults with CKD, the 2025 guideline recommends a BP goal < 130/80 mmHg [
1
]. An ACE inhibitor or ARB is preferred when albuminuria or another kidney-specific indication is present because renin–angiotensin system blockade can reduce albuminuria and slow kidney disease progression in appropriately selected patients.
UACR should be measured to identify kidney injury and guide therapy. Serum creatinine and potassium should be reassessed after initiation or dose escalation of renin–angiotensin system blockade. A modest early increase in creatinine may reflect the expected hemodynamic effect of treatment and does not necessarily require discontinuation. In contrast, a substantial or progressive decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate should prompt evaluation for volume depletion, concomitant medications, renovascular disease, or another underlying process rather than automatic continuation or withdrawal of therapy.
The 2021 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guideline recommends an SBP target < 120 mmHg for many adults with nondialysis CKD when BP is measured using a standardized office protocol [
42
]. This recommendation is broadly compatible with the 2025 guideline’s encouragement of lower SBP values in selected high-risk patients, but the measurement requirement is critical. Dialysis patients, kidney transplant recipients, and individuals with advanced frailty remain underrepresented in intensive-treatment trials, and targets in these populations require greater individualization.
Diabetes
Diabetes is now incorporated directly into the higher-risk treatment pathway. Adults with diabetes should begin antihypertensive medication at an average BP ≥ 130/80 mmHg, with a treatment goal < 130/80 mmHg [
1
]. An SBP < 120 mmHg may be considered in appropriately selected patients when the expected cardiovascular benefit outweighs treatment burden and the risks of hypotension, electrolyte abnormalities, or kidney injury.
Initial drug selection follows the same race-neutral framework used for other adults with hypertension. An ACE inhibitor or ARB is particularly appropriate when albuminuric CKD is present, whereas treatment in the absence of albuminuria can be selected from the usual first-line classes according to comorbidities, tolerance, and patient preference.
BPROAD substantially narrows the uncertainty left by ACCORD-BP by demonstrating cardiovascular benefit from intensive SBP lowering in high-risk adults with type-2 diabetes. However, its participants were treated entirely in China and had elevated baseline cardiovascular risk. The findings therefore strongly support intensive treatment in comparable high-risk patients but should not be extrapolated automatically to younger adults with diabetes, low short-term cardiovascular risk, or a greater susceptibility to treatment-related adverse effects. BP treatment remains indicated, but its intensity should be individualized.
Conclusions
The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline retains the 2017 BP categories while advancing risk-based and earlier treatment. PREVENT now guides risk assessment, while persistent lower-risk stage-1 hypertension prompts medication after structured lifestyle therapy. Stage-2 hypertension generally begins with a two-drug single-pill combination. UACR testing and broader primary aldosteronism screening move target-organ and secondary-cause evaluation earlier in care. Renal denervation enters the algorithm as a limited adjunct rather than a substitute for optimized therapy. Evidence increasingly supports lower SBP targets in selected older adults and patients with diabetes or CKD, but benefit depends on standardized measurement, tolerability, and safety monitoring. The principal challenge is no longer identifying effective treatments, but delivering them consistently through timely intensification, home monitoring, team-based care, and reliable follow-up.
